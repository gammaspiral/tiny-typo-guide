Typography / Design System

A practical system for pairing type.

Typography works when hierarchy, contrast and rhythm are intentional. You do not need a serif and a sans-serif to create good typography. You need clearly differentiated roles.

Principles Pairings Scale Spacing Weights Rules Recipes

The core principles

Think of typefaces as roles in a system rather than isolated choices.

01

Contrast creates hierarchy.

Headlines should not compete with body copy. Use differences in size, weight, width, spacing or typeface to establish levels of importance.

02

Harmony prevents chaos.

Contrasting typefaces should still share some underlying character: proportion, era, geometry, stroke quality or overall tone.

03

Body text has a different job.

Display type attracts attention. Body type must remain comfortable across many lines. Prioritize readability over novelty.

04

Use fewer typefaces than you think.

One family with several weights can create a complete system. Two well-chosen families are usually enough.

05

Hierarchy matters more than font pairing.

A beautifully paired typeface system can still fail if everything is the same size, weight and spacing.

Four fundamental pairings

There is no universal “correct” relationship between serif and sans-serif. Each combination produces a different voice.

01 / Sans + Serif
Modern headline
This combination is especially effective for editorial, cultural and sophisticated layouts. The sans-serif establishes a clean visual entry point while the serif provides comfortable reading texture.
02 / Sans + Sans
Modern system
A sans-serif headline and sans-serif body can be exceptionally clean. Create hierarchy through weight, scale, width and spacing rather than relying on a serif/sans contrast.
03 / Serif + Sans
Editorial voice
A serif headline gives the composition personality while the sans-serif body provides a functional, contemporary reading experience.
04 / Serif + Serif
Literary system
Serif-on-serif works when the two roles are clearly differentiated. Use different weights, proportions or sizes rather than two nearly identical faces.

Build a type scale

Start with a readable body size and scale upward from there. Exact values depend on the medium, typeface and viewing distance.

DISPLAY / 64–96px
A strong opening statement.
H1 / 40–56px
Primary heading
H2 / 28–36px
Section heading
LEAD / 19–22px
Introductory or emphasized text.
BODY / 16–18px
Standard body text should be comfortable for sustained reading.
CAPTION / 12–14px
Supporting information and metadata.

Spacing and measure

Typography is not just letters. The empty space around and between them is part of the design.

Body leading 1.45–1.7×
Headline leading 0.95–1.15×
Body measure 45–75 characters
Paragraph gap 0.8–1.2× line-height


TIP

Long lines make reading harder.

On desktop, avoid letting body copy stretch across the entire screen. A narrower measure usually feels more intentional and is easier to read.

Use weight strategically

Weight is one of the easiest ways to establish hierarchy without introducing another typeface.

Regular / 400 Primary body text
Medium / 500 Navigation / emphasis
Semibold / 600 Subheadings / labels
Bold / 700 Major headings
RULE

Do not make everything bold.

If everything has emphasis, nothing has emphasis. Reserve heavy weights for moments where the hierarchy genuinely needs them.

Do / don't

Do
Create contrast.

Make the relationship between headline, subheading and body immediately understandable.

Don't
Create noise.

Avoid combining several decorative typefaces simply because each one looks interesting on its own.

Do
Pair by character.

Look at proportions, x-height, stroke contrast, width and overall personality—not just serif versus sans-serif.

Don't
Pair by category alone.

“One serif + one sans” does not automatically make a good pairing.

Practical recipes

These are starting points, not rigid rules.

Editorial
Sans display + Serif body
Tight headline leading + generous body leading
Strong size contrast

Modern / Minimal
Sans headline + Sans body
Weight contrast + restrained spacing
One type family is often enough

Luxury / Cultural
Serif headline + Sans body
Large display size + generous whitespace
Light or regular body weight

Traditional / Literary
Serif headline + Serif body
Moderate contrast between weights and sizes
Long-form reading measure


FINAL

When in doubt, start with one family.

Choose a typeface with enough weights and styles to handle the entire hierarchy. Introduce a second family only when it adds a meaningful contrast in voice or function.

Typography System

Hierarchy first. Pairing second.

The best typography systems feel inevitable: the reader always knows what to look at, what to read next, and what information matters most.
